How it began

One mother's idea. An entire school's culture.

In the fall of 2002, Kim Piro was volunteering in her daughter Jamie's classroom when she noticed that typically developing children were unsure of how to interact with Jamie, who has autism. A former psychiatric social worker, Kim asked the teacher if she could come in and read Captain Tommy — a story written to help children understand autism spectrum disorder. The discussion that followed was extraordinary.

The next time Kim visited, she saw a clear difference in how Jamie's classmates treated her. Jamie's condition was becoming less of a barrier. The children couldn't wait to share their successful attempts to engage her. The iCare program was born.

The program

What happens in the classroom

Students with special needs are increasingly integrated into general education classrooms — and their classmates naturally have many questions. iCare was created to answer those questions, often before they're ever asked.

Trained volunteers read from an approved book list and then engage students and teachers in discussion about each story. iCare creates a friendly, safe environment where thoughtful questions can be asked and answered openly. Every reading volunteer receives story-specific question and answer cards attached to the back cover of each book.

Topics range from general differences to specific disabilities including autism and Down syndrome. In younger grades, stories focus on broad themes of differences and acceptance. With older students, discussions address specific disabilities more directly — and children at every age are eager to participate.

What volunteers say

From the people who've been in the room

iCare began in Mansfield and has run there for over twenty years. These reflections come from volunteers and school leaders who helped build it.

“The literature chosen for the program is very well written and age appropriate. In all age groups, I have found the children to be interested in the stories, and very willing to discuss their own personal experiences.”

— iCare Volunteer

“They were always eager to ask questions, usually in a way that made me feel they had been wondering about the answers for a while. It was very rewarding to provide an opportunity for typical children to learn about their special needs peers.”

— iCare Volunteer & Special Needs Parent

“All students should be accepted and celebrated for who they are. Through literature and classroom discussions, the iCare program exposes all of our students to conversations about these very topics.”

— Mansfield Elementary School Assistant Principal

“The program has grown from a single concept to one that has become part of the culture in the Mansfield Public Schools. It is without reservation that I endorse the iCare Program.”

— Mansfield Public Schools Superintendent
